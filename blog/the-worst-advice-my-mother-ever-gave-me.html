Personal Essay

The Worst Advice My Mother Ever Gave Me

Sometimes the people who believe in us the most unintentionally give us permission to quit.

Growing up, my mom had a thing for buying shitty pop-up campers.

She loved camping. We all did.

But these weren't exactly luxury accommodations.

One particular camper she bought for about $500 needed some serious work. The canvas was ripped and needed sewing. The walls were filthy. The floors looked like they hadn't seen a mop in years.

And in our house, fixing it up was an all-hands-on-deck operation.

I was 10 years old.

I remember turning 10 because my mom made a big deal about it. Double digits. A big milestone. More responsibility.

Apparently, that responsibility included scrubbing the hell out of a camper floor.

So there I was, on my hands and knees, trying to clean what I assumed was once white linoleum.

Except no matter how much I scrubbed, it was still black.

The dirt wasn't budging. The stains weren't disappearing. And I was getting frustrated.

Finally, I looked at my mom and said,

“Mom, I can't get it clean.”

She looked at me and said something that probably sounded like perfectly reasonable parenting advice.

“Just do the best you can.”

And just like that, I had my out.

I was done.

Not because the floor was clean.

Not because I had exhausted every possible option.

But because someone had given me permission to decide I'd done enough.

I told myself I'd done my best.

But had I?

That's the part I've never forgotten.

Sure, I was 10. What did I know about cleaning decades of dirt off cheap linoleum?

Maybe that floor was never going to come clean.

Maybe I could have scrubbed for another three hours and gotten nowhere.

But here's what I remember.

I knew I could have tried harder.

I could have tried something different. Asked for another cleaning product. Changed my approach.

Instead, I stopped.

Because “I did my best” was easier to accept than admitting I didn't want to keep trying.

And for some reason, that moment has stayed with me for more than 30 years.

When Encouragement Becomes Permission

Think about how often we hear these words as adults.

“You're doing the best you can.”

“You're trying, and that's what matters.”

“Don't be so hard on yourself.”

We say these things to people we love because we want to support them.

And sometimes, they're exactly what someone needs to hear.

But sometimes?

They're an escape hatch.

A way to make ourselves feel better about something we know we could have approached differently.

Maybe you're struggling at work.

Maybe you're trying to build a business.

Maybe you're working toward a goal that seems further away every time you take a step toward it.

And someone who loves you tells you, “You're doing the best you can.”

You smile. You appreciate the encouragement.

But somewhere in the back of your mind, there's a question.

Am I?

Not “Am I working hard?”

Not “Am I exhausted?”

Not “Am I doing more than everyone else?”

Am I genuinely giving this everything I'm capable of giving it?

Or have I simply reached the point where continuing feels uncomfortable?

There's a difference.

Your Best Isn't Always Your Limit

I've spent years in leadership, coaching people, developing teams, and pushing others to accomplish things they didn't initially believe they could.

And one thing I've learned is that people are often capable of more than they think.

But there's a catch.

You can't always recognize that potential from a place of comfort.

Sometimes you have to challenge your own assumptions.

Sometimes you have to admit that the approach you're taking isn't working.

And sometimes you have to ask yourself whether you're truly at your limit or simply tired of being challenged.

That doesn't mean working yourself into the ground.

It doesn't mean ignoring your mental health, your family, or your responsibilities.

And it certainly doesn't mean that every failure is the result of not trying hard enough.

Some things genuinely are outside our control.

That camper floor might have been one of them.

But the outcome and the effort are two different things.

I couldn't necessarily control whether that floor came clean.

I could control whether I tried another approach before deciding I was finished.

And that's the distinction I wish I'd understood at 10 years old.

The One Person Who Knows

Your support system matters.

Surround yourself with people who encourage you, believe in you, and remind you of your worth when things get difficult.

But understand something.

Those people don't live inside your head.

They don't know every opportunity you passed up.

Every time you could have pushed a little further.

Every moment you chose the comfortable option over the difficult one.

They see what you show them.

And because they love you, they're often going to give you the benefit of the doubt.

That's not a bad thing.

But encouragement and accountability aren't the same thing.

The people around you can believe in you. Only you can be completely honest with yourself about your effort.

My mom wasn't trying to teach me to quit.

She was trying to teach a frustrated 10-year-old that sometimes doing your best is enough.

And she wasn't wrong.

But what I took from that moment was something different.

I learned how easy it was to use those words as permission to stop.

More than 30 years later, I still think about that filthy camper floor.

Not because I wish I'd gotten it clean.

But because I remember knowing I had more to give.

And choosing not to.

The CC Way

The next time someone tells you, “You're doing the best you can,” appreciate their support.

Then ask yourself one question.

“Am I doing my best, or am I doing the best I'm willing to do right now?”

Because those aren't always the same thing.

And nobody else can answer that question for you.

Now I'm curious. Have you ever caught yourself using “I'm doing my best” as an excuse to stop trying?
